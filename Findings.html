Who Owns Nashville? Institutional Ownership, Financing, and Construction of Single-Family Housing in Davidson County, Tennessee

Natalie Baldacci

Who Owns Nashville project

July 2026

Abstract

Institutional investors that buy, build, and rent out single-family homes have continued to expand their presence since the Great Recession. This paper documents their footprint in Davidson County, Tennessee, using county administrative records alone: the Metro Nashville parcel roll and parcel history, Register of Deeds instruments, building permits, state business filings, landlord registrations, SEC disclosures, and HUD administrative rent and income series. We construct a baseline of 206,065 single-family homes and consolidate shell entities to ultimate parents using conservative record linkage. We document five facts. First, national single-family rental operators hold 1.9 percent of the county's homes, but their holdings concentrate sharply: institutional share exceeds 13 percent in several southeast census tracts, reaches 70 percent of one block group, and covers entire subdivisions. Second, 118 securitization deals pledge 1,833 Davidson homes as bond collateral, with a median holding period of six years and counting. Third, build-to-rent construction has become the dominant entry channel, and 92.8 percent of tracked build-to-rent parcels remain held or in the pipeline. Fourth, the development pipeline rests on land held almost entirely by out-of-state capital. Fifth, the HUD two-bedroom Fair Market Rent rose 80 percent over 2017-2026 while median family income rose 69 percent, leaving an extremely-low-income family 859 dollars per month short of the official rent. The same southeast geography carries every layer.

Keywords: single-family rentals, institutional investors, build-to-rent, securitization, Nashville

1 Introduction

A new class of residential arrangement emerged after the foreclosure crisis: single-family rentals owned, operated, and managed by large financial firms. A growing literature examines these firms at the national level. Lee and Wylie (2024) find that investor share is higher in neighborhoods with lower housing values and higher shares of Black and non-college residents, and that investors raise rents at 60 percent above the average rate when first acquiring a property. Gorback, Qian, and Zhu (2024) document that long-term rental companies outgrew every other investor type over the last decade, that they concentrate their holdings in select census tracts, and that growth in their local market share leads to measurably faster house price and rent growth. Goodman and Zinn (2023) supply the standard taxonomy of build-to-rent delivery models.

This paper brings that national picture down to a single county with unusually complete public records. Rather than relying on proprietary transaction data, we build the entire analysis from primary government sources: the Metro Nashville cadastral parcel roll and parcel history service, recorded deeds and deeds of trust at the Davidson County Register of Deeds, the Metro ePermits system, Tennessee Secretary of State filings, Metro landlord registrations, SEC EDGAR exhibits, and the HUD Fair Market Rent and Income Limits series. Every figure in this paper links to an interactive map or table that carries it, and each page cites its source and, where possible, a replayable query.

We document five sets of facts. Section 3 shows that institutional ownership is modest countywide, at 1.9 percent of the single-family stock, but extreme where it lands, reaching 70 percent of one block group. Section 4 traces the financing: 118 securitization deals pledge 1,833 Davidson homes as bond collateral, and the paper trail runs from SEC exhibits to individual county deeds. Section 5 examines build-to-rent construction, now the principal entry channel, and shows that nearly all of it remains in rental hands. Section 6 documents the land pipeline, held almost entirely by out-of-state capital. Section 7 sets the affordability backdrop using HUD's own administrative series. Section 8 concludes. Our evidence is descriptive; we do not estimate causal effects. But the geographic pattern we document, in which one southeast crescent of the county carries the institutional share, the build-to-rent communities, the securitized collateral, the never-occupied new construction, and the recorded code violations at once, is consistent with the tract-level concentration that Gorback, Qian, and Zhu (2024) identify nationally and with the entry-neighborhood profile in Lee and Wylie (2024).

2 Data and Definitions

The unit of analysis is the parcel. We define the framework baseline as every Davidson County parcel with one or two dwelling units and a land use of Single Family, Residential Condo, Duplex, or Zero Lot Line: 206,065 homes as of July 2026 (the framework file, reconcilable against live county queries on that page). All ownership shares in this paper use this baseline as the denominator.

Owner attribution proceeds in two steps. First, owner names and mailing addresses on the parcel roll are cleaned and deduplicated. Second, entities are consolidated to ultimate parents using the record-linkage approach of Hangen and O'Brien (2024) and the Who Owns Atlanta project: standardized name stems, TF-IDF string similarity, shared non-agent mailing addresses with exclusions for high-degree hub addresses and registered-agent offices, Tennessee Secretary of State principal and mailing addresses, and landlord-registration contacts, which since 2018 must carry a real mailing address rather than a post office box. Ambiguous merges are flagged for review rather than forced. The resulting graph is browsable in the owner directory.

We define institutional operators as the national single-family rental parents holding 350 or more homes, following the threshold in the proposed ROAD to Housing Act; the framework file attributes 3,940 homes to them. The broader corporate category, any home held through a business entity, covers 16,613 homes. We use HUD entity 4703799999 (the Nashville MSA) for Fair Market Rents and Income Limits, program years 2017-2026, and ACS 2020-2024 five-year estimates for tract context.

3 The Ownership Landscape

3.1 Countywide scale

Table 1 reports the headline counts. Of 206,042 baseline homes assignable to a census tract, 42,605 (20.7 percent) are not occupied by their owner, 16,613 (8.1 percent) are held through a corporate entity, 11,932 (5.8 percent) are held by an out-of-state owner, and 3,940 (1.9 percent) are held by an identified national operator. The two most quoted numbers, 1.9 percent institutional and 8.1 percent corporate, answer different questions, and public discussion regularly conflates them.

3.2 Concentration

The countywide share understates the phenomenon where it operates. Institutional share exceeds 13 percent of all baseline homes in the highest census tract (Tract 015630, 297 of 2,204 homes), reaches 70.2 percent of block group 0156291 (59 of 84 homes), and covers 100 percent of individual subdivisions (the institutional share map). By community, Antioch leads at 14.8 percent, driven by Progress Residential, and Hermitage at 9.1 percent, driven by American Homes 4 Rent (by community). This within-county concentration mirrors the national tract-level clustering in Gorback, Qian, and Zhu (2024). A countywide average and a block-group reality are different facts, and policy calibrated to one will miss the other.

3.3 Consolidation to parents

On paper the holdings belong to hundreds of shell LLCs: Yamasa Co., ARMM Asset Company 2, SFR JV-1 Borrower, and their peers. Consolidated, the corrected leaderboard reads Progress Residential 1,215 homes, American Homes 4 Rent 1,182, Amherst 699, and Regent 642 (leaderboard). Shared mail hubs tie the same parents to the Atlanta market: 5001 Plaza on the Lake (Pretium/Progress), 15771 Red Hill Avenue (Tricon), and 16220 North Scottsdale Road (Invitation Homes) anchor holdings in both cities.

4 Financing: The Homes as Collateral

The rental stock is not only owned; it is pledged. We identify 118 securitization deals with Davidson homes in their collateral pools, touching 1,833 homes, each traceable from the SEC exhibit that disclosed it to the quitclaim deed and deed of trust recorded at the county (Who Finances Nashville; document trail). The same deals pledge 4,879 homes across five states (national map). Two patterns stand out in the deal records. First, appreciation accrues inside the bond structure: the Davidson collateral of ARP 2014-1 was appraised at 7.7 million dollars at closing and 17.4 million dollars now, an increase of 128 percent. Second, the homes do not churn. The median securitized home has been held six years and counting, consistent with the rent stream, rather than resale, being the asset: the median home sits in a 2,390 dollar per month three-bedroom fair-market-rent area. These are the rent-backed securities described in Lee and Wylie (2024), observed here at the level of individual recorded instruments.

5 Build-to-Rent: The New Entry Channel

5.1 Delivery models

Scattered-site acquisition of existing homes dominated the 2012-2021 wave. The current channel is construction. Following Goodman and Zinn (2023), we sort every build-to-rent parcel by who owns the land during construction. Table 2 reports the result.

The flow-through confirms the classification: of every tracked build-to-rent parcel, 92.8 percent remains held or in the development pipeline and 7.2 percent ever reached a homebuyer (models and impact). None of this construction is barred by the ROAD to Housing Act, which regulates the resale channel rather than new construction.

5.2 The never-occupied stock

Deed history offers an independent check. We identify 1,992 single-family homes built since 2010 in which no owner in the chain of title ever had a mailing address matching the property (time-lapse map). Of these, 636 sit with a named national operator, and 577 of the 636 belong to American Homes 4 Rent. That single number is the signature of the one large operator that builds and retains at scale in this county.

5.3 Legal form

The prevailing legal vehicle matters for oversight. Build-to-rent communities here are typically recorded as horizontal property regimes rather than subdivisions. The form conveys each home as a volume of space over commonly held land, which is why one operator can hold 199 homes on zero recorded acres, and, under Tennessee Attorney General Opinion 01-147, establishing a regime is not a subdivision and so escapes lot-size, frontage, and planning-approval requirements (the players, with statutory citations). Two local builders, Elmington Residential and Ole South Properties, supply most of the pipeline across several competing operators.

6 The Land Pipeline

Upstream of construction, the land is banked. Not one of the ten largest out-of-state residential landholders in the county mails its tax bill to a Tennessee address, and seven of the top thirteen mail to two cities, Scottsdale, Arizona and Calabasas, California. Dedicated land-bank vehicles hold platted lots for builder clients under recorded option agreements: TPG/Angelo Gordon with 169 parcels, Kennedy Lewis with 131, Domain, and Walton Global's 211 raw acres (vacant land; Walton tracts; land over time). The resale channel shows the same direction of capital: 17,093 existing homes have been bought arms-length by out-of-state owners since 2000, with a pronounced surge in 2021 (time-lapse; mailing addresses).

7 Rents and Affordability

HUD's administrative series supplies the backdrop. The Nashville two-bedroom Fair Market Rent rose from 959 dollars in 2017 to 1,730 dollars in 2026, an increase of 80 percent, while median family income rose from 68,700 to 116,100 dollars, an increase of 69 percent. At the bottom of the distribution the arithmetic no longer closes: a four-person household at the extremely-low-income limit (34,850 dollars in 2026) can afford 871 dollars per month at the 30 percent standard, 859 dollars short of the Fair Market Rent, which would consume 60 percent of its income. Nor is the county one market: 2026 two-bedroom Small Area rents range from 970 to 2,600 dollars across its ZIP codes, a 2.7-fold spread that maps onto the ownership geography (rent vs. income). Lee and Wylie (2024) find that investor presence is associated with faster rent increases even for non-investor landlords; our administrative rent series cannot test that mechanism, but the level pattern is consistent with it.

8 Conclusion

Every layer documented above concentrates in the same place: the southeast crescent of Antioch, Cane Ridge, and Hermitage along Interstate 24. That crescent holds the county's highest institutional shares, its build-to-rent communities, its securitized collateral, its never-occupied new construction, its out-of-state buyer surge, and the recorded property-standards violations of its institutional landlords (violations map). The area is where the county built new, uniform, relatively affordable single-family supply, and precisely those characteristics, which Gorback, Qian, and Zhu (2024) formalize as suitability for long-term rental operators, are what drew every stage of the pipeline to it. The map of who owns Nashville is, at bottom, a map of where Nashville built starter homes.

Three caveats bound the findings. The evidence is descriptive, not causal. The counts are snapshots, dated on each page. And the never-occupied figure (1,992 homes) is this project's reproduction of an analysis first run against the live parcel service (2,405 homes); the method note on that map states both figures. Extensions in progress include a multi-county comparison with the Atlanta market, with which Nashville already shares its operator mail hubs, and a time-series version of the ownership choropleths using the 2011-2024 ACS series.
