The statute

The 21st Century Housing Act

Section 1001 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — Homes Are for People, Not Corporations — for fifteen years bars large institutional investors from buying single-family homes, then lists the purchases that survive the ban. This page files every numbered line of § 1001 under the Part of the series where it does its work, and explains in plain English what each provision does. The genuinely cross-cutting lines are flagged.

The seam

The Act splits along the same lifecycle the series walks backwards. Its ban lands downstream, where institutions buy existing homes to rent. Its exceptions bless the upstream end, where they build new homes to rent. Land banking sits in a blind spot, and financing cuts across everything. The statute is precise where the actors are clear and goes blind exactly where they turn murky.

PART I

Who Owns Nashville?

Definitional spine · the ban · the meta-provisions
Part I introduces the cast and the statute's machinery: the definitions everything else runs on, the ban itself, and the study and effective-date provisions. The lettered exceptions live here as an umbrella, then get distributed to the Parts where they operate.
SEC. 1001Homes Are for People, Not Corporations
The whole section. For fifteen years, large institutional investors may not buy single-family homes unless a listed exception applies.
§ 1001(a)Definitions
The terms that control everything. The ban and its exceptions mean only what these definitions say.
§ 1001(a)(2)Excepted Purchase
The master list of purchases still allowed despite the ban. The most consequential definition in the section, because everything an institution may still do lives here. The lettered carve-outs are distributed to their Parts below.
§ 1001(a)(2)(K)Series of purchases under (A)–(J)
An exception still applies whether the institution buys one home or does a bundle or a series. No one loses an exception just because it is part of a larger deal.
§ 1001(a)(2)(I)Purchase from non-covered investor
A two-year grace window after the ban starts, letting deals already in motion with smaller sellers close. also a timing lever in Landbanks
§ 1001(a)(3)Single-Family Home
The protected asset: a structure with two or fewer dwelling units, excluding manufactured homes. Duplexes are inside the definition; quadruplexes and mobile homes are not.
§ 1001(a)(3)(A)
Structure with two or fewer dwelling units.
§ 1001(a)(3)(B)
Manufactured-home exclusion.
§ 1001(a)(4)Large Institutional Investor
Who the ban targets: a for-profit entity in the business of owning or renting single-family homes that controls 350 or more of them. The 350 line is low enough to sweep in mid-size private operators, not just the Big Four.
§ 1001(a)(4)(A)(i)
For-profit entity controlling 350 or more single-family homes.
§ 1001(a)(4)(A)(ii)
Government-entity exclusion.
§ 1001(a)(4)(B)Rule of construction — control
Defines control broadly so investors cannot hide behind shells. This is the statutory version of the parent-brand resolution this project already performs (Yamasa → Progress, VB TAH → VineBrook).
§ 1001(a)(4)(B)(i)–(v)
You control a home if you own it or hold fiduciary authority (i); control the general partner or managing member (ii); control the investment manager or advisor (iii); own more than 25% of any equity class unless a passive investor (iv); or otherwise control the owning entity (v). (iv) also applies in Finances
§ 1001(a)(5)Purchase
Defined broadly to include mergers, acquisitions, construction, foreclosures, and bulk buys, cash or not. You cannot dodge the ban by calling an acquisition a merger. cross-referenced in Builds — "construction" sits inside this definition
§ 1001(b)Prohibition on Purchases
The operative ban. (1) no large institutional investor may buy a single-family home; (2) exceptions, including restructuring homes already owned at enactment; (3) no forced divestiture and no interference with bankruptcy; (4) Treasury may implement but not amend the definitions or thresholds.
§ 1001(e)Studies
GAO reports on housing availability, affordability, and whether the ban cut institutional demand; HUD reports on definitional adjustments and legislative recommendations; a Sense of Congress on intent.
§ 1001(f)Effective Date
Takes effect 180 days after enactment and is repealed fifteen years after the effective date.
PART II

Who Rents Nashville?

The ban lands here · renter-facing · rent-to-own
Part II is where the prohibition actually bites: the existing home bought to rent, held by a traditional operator. The renter-protection machinery and the rent-to-own carve-outs live here.
§ 1001(b)The Prohibition, applied
Introduced in Part I; analyzed here, where it lands. The buy-existing-to-rent model is exactly what the ban targets. the ban
§ 1001(a)(1)Consumer Reporting Agency
Operative only through the positive-rent-reporting carve-outs below.
§ 1001(a)(2)(D)Homeownership Program
An institution may buy and rent a home inside a genuine rent-to-own program, subject to four guardrails: rent and fees capped at comparable non-program levels (i); the deal treated as a dwelling-secured consumer credit contract so consumer-protection law attaches (ii); on-time rent reported to credit bureaus with renter opt-in (iii); and real money toward the eventual purchase (iv). (ii) also applies in Finances
§ 1001(a)(2)(E)Program to Boost Homeownership
A parallel path: positive rent reporting with opt-in (i); a right of first refusal and 30-day "first look" for the tenant (ii); and optional financial support or price concessions (iii).
§ 1001(a)(2)(J)55-and-Older Community
Purchases tied to age-restricted communities are excepted.
§ 1001(c)Renter Outreach Resource
Establishes a renter-facing resource that monitors and resolves disputes, investigates potential federal-law violations, notifies renters at occupancy and annually, reports to Congress each year with anonymized dispute data, and requires investors to notify Treasury of their status and the count and location of their homes.
PART III

Who Builds Nashville?

The construction exception
Part III is the carve-out. The Act bans buying existing homes but explicitly blesses building new ones, which pushes institutional capital toward construction — exactly where the build-to-rent data already points.
§ 1001(a)(2)(A)Build, renovate, or convert for sale
An institution may build or renovate a home if the purpose is to sell it, not rent it. It cannot quietly rent the home while it "waits" to sell. This protects for-sale homebuilding. exception
§ 1001(a)(2)(B)Build-to-Rent Program
The central provision for this Part. An institution may build new homes specifically to rent them, in an all-rental or a mixed community. The law bans buying existing homes but expressly permits building new rental stock. exception
§ 1001(a)(2)(C)Renovate-to-Rent Program
An institution may buy a substandard home, rehab it, and rent it, but only if the home genuinely fails building code (i) and the improvements cost at least 15% of the purchase price (ii). A floor that proves the rehab is real, not cosmetic.
§ 1001(a)(5)Purchase — cross-reference
"Construction" sits inside the definition of purchase, which is precisely why a build-to-rent exception has to exist. defined in Owns
PART IV

Who Landbanks Nashville?

The Act's blind spot
Part IV lives in an absence. Raw land and unbuilt lots are not yet single-family homes, so the ban never reaches the upstream land stage. That gap is the Part's central finding.
No dedicated provision
Land is not a "home" under § 1001(a)(3), so a large investor may acquire and hold developable land freely. The statute goes silent exactly where capital moves upstream. the blind spot
Seven-year disposal requirement
A holding-period limit attaches to certain excepted categories. VERIFY — pin to the engrossed bill; this figure comes from the Capright May 2026 summary, not the section text
§ 1001(a)(2)(I)2-year non-covered-seller window
Read here as a timing and transition lever on when banked land can change hands. defined in Owns
PART V

Who Finances Nashville?

Capital · lenders · enforcement
Part V follows the capital. Financing here means acquisition and refinancing across every operator type, focused on the securitization of single-family rents. The lender and enforcement carve-outs, and the control tests, converge on this stage.
§ 1001(a)(2)(F)Debts previously contracted
A purchase to satisfy a debt contracted before enactment is excepted.
§ 1001(a)(2)(G)Servicer / lender loss mitigation
A lender or servicer may take a home through foreclosure (i), deed-in-lieu (ii), enforcement of a mortgage, deed of trust, or security interest (iii), or operation of law after borrower default (iv).
§ 1001(a)(2)(H)Purchase from compliant investor
One institution may buy homes from another if the seller already held them at enactment or bought them legally under the Act. Institution-to-institution transfers add no new homes to the pile, so they are allowed. Portfolio trades run through here.
§ 1001(d)Enforcement
Civil penalties (1), with proceeds transferred to HUD for homeownership-expansion activities (2).
§ 1001(a)(4)(B)(iv)25%-equity / passive-investor line
A capital-stack control question: who counts as controlling an owning entity through equity. defined in Owns
§ 1001(b)(3)(B)Bankruptcy carve-out
The ban does not interfere with Title 11 bankruptcy proceedings. defined in Owns
§ 1001(a)(2)(D)(ii)Dwelling-secured consumer credit contract
The rent-to-own deal treated as consumer mortgage credit. defined in Rents

What counts as a "single-family home": the Act vs. the Census Bureau

Structure type21st Century Housing Act § 1001(a)(3)Census Bureau, Survey of Construction
Detached houseSingle-family — 1 unitSingle-family
Duplex (2 units)Single-family — 2 or fewer unitsSingle-family (attached)
Townhouse / row houseSingle-family only if 2 or fewer units in the structureSingle-family, if separated ground-to-roof and with its own utilities
Quadruplex (4 units)Not single-family — exceeds 2 unitsSingle-family under Census
Manufactured / mobile homeExpressly excludedCounted separately (not site-built)
Condominium (horizontal property regime)Turns on dwelling units in the structure, not on the plat — a Tennessee horizontal-property condo can look single-family but be one common-ownership platClassified by structure, not ownership form

Why the gap matters. The Census single-family definition folds in quadruplexes and townhouse rows that the Act's two-unit ceiling excludes. Any count built on the Census definition overstates the stock the ban actually protects. In Tennessee, the horizontal property regime lets condos read as single-family homes on a parcel while sitting on one common-ownership plat, which changes how holdings must be counted. The Act resolves the long-running research dispute by fixing the line at two units and excluding manufactured homes.
